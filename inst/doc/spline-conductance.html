1 Why splines for conductance?

The default terradish conductance model is log-linear:

\[C_i = \exp(\theta_1 x_{i1} + \theta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots)\]

This model represents a monotone fitted association for each landscape covariate: does relative conductance go up or down as the covariate increases, conditional on the other predictors? That constrained form is useful when a monotone relationship is scientifically defensible.

But ecological responses to landscape features are often more complex. Consider:

  • Elevation: A prior hypothesis may predict the strongest connectivity at intermediate elevations rather than at either extreme.
  • Slope: A prior hypothesis may predict little change until terrain passes a threshold.
  • Temperature: Many ectotherms have a thermal optimum; conductance may be highest at intermediate temperatures and fall off steeply in both directions.
  • Canopy density: A prior hypothesis may predict lower connectivity at both low and high canopy density.

None of these shapes can be captured by a single linear term on the log scale. A negative coefficient gives a declining fitted conductance association; a positive coefficient gives an increasing association. There is no room for an interior optimum.

smooth_loglinear_conductance() extends the log-linear model by expanding selected covariates into spline basis functions. The model structure is still:

\[C_i = \exp(B_i \boldsymbol{\theta})\]

where \(B_i\) is a row of the expanded design matrix for raster cell \(i\). For covariates not wrapped in s(), \(B_i\) contains the raw value. For covariates wrapped in s(), \(B_i\) contains several basis-function evaluations instead of one raw value. The optimizer still sees an ordinary log-linear model; the splines live entirely inside the design matrix, which is computed once and then treated as fixed.

2 What are spline basis functions?

A spline is a piecewise polynomial function that is constrained to be smooth (continuous and differentiable) at the join points, called knots. Instead of estimating a single slope for altitude, we estimate a weighted combination of several basis functions, each capturing part of the altitude range.

The figure below shows what natural spline and B-spline basis functions look like for a covariate that runs from −2 to 2 (as after scale_covariates()):

library(splines)
library(terradish)

# Natural spline basis with df = 4
B_ns <- ns(x_seq, df = 4)

# B-spline basis with k = 4 columns, degree 3 (cubic)
B_bs <- bs(x_seq, df = 4, degree = 3)

par(mfrow = c(1, 2), mar = c(4, 4, 3, 1))

Natural spline (ns) and B-spline (bs) basis functions for a standardized covariate. Each colored line is one basis column. The fitted conductance association is a weighted combination of all basis functions in a panel.

Each basis column covers a different part of the covariate range. The optimizer assigns a coefficient to each column; the weighted sum of all columns defines the shape of the association. A hump in the middle of the covariate range is captured by upweighting the middle basis columns relative to the edge ones.

2.1 Natural splines vs. B-splines

Both produce smooth curves, but they differ in behavior outside the observed data range:

Property Natural spline (ns) B-spline (bs)
Behavior at edges Linear beyond boundary knots Polynomial (degree-dependent); can extrapolate wildly
Partition of unity No Yes (basis sums to 1 everywhere)
Parameter count for df = k k columns k columns (knots placed to give k)
Default degree N/A (cubic, fixed) Cubic (degree = 3); user-adjustable
Best for General use; safe extrapolation When you know the curve may be non-linear at the edges too

For most landscape-genetics applications, natural splines are likely the safer default. The landscape feature you observe at sampling sites is what matters; extrapolation is usually not meaningful for raster-based conductance models because the graph only covers cells with observed covariate values. Still, B-splines are useful when you have strong prior knowledge that the edge behavior is non-linear, or when you want finer control over the spline degree.

2.2 How many basis functions (df / k)?

More basis functions → more shape flexibility → more parameters to estimate. With sparse spatial genetic data, overfitting is a real risk. Guidelines:

  • df = 3 or 4 is a good starting point for most covariates. This allows a single hump, valley, or threshold-like shape.
  • df = 2 is a low-flexibility nonlinear curve; it adds one parameter over the linear model and can represent simple curvature.
  • df ≥ 6 should be reserved for cases where both the data and a clear ecological hypothesis support it; compare against simpler models with AICc.
  • Consider AICc when the number of focal sites is small relative to the number of estimated parameters. terradish uses focal sites, not the number of dependent population pairs, as n in its AICc calculation.

4 Checking settings before spline fitting

terradish_assess_settings() is worth calling before fitting spline conductance models for an additional reason beyond raw graph size: spline formulas expand the conductance parameter count. A formula with s(altitude, df = 4) has four basis coefficients for altitude alone, plus any other terms. With enough parameters, Newton-Raphson can become slower than BFGS per iteration. The assessment handles this automatically; it probes the expanded model, not the raw formula, and reports the smooth-term expansion in the $profile and in its notes.

# Assessment for the altitude natural-spline model (df = 4)
# Uses the same surface built above (saveStack = TRUE required for solver probes)
assessment_spline <- terradish_assess_settings(
  melip.Fst ~ forestcover + s(altitude, df = 4),
  data              = surface,
  conductance_model = smooth_loglinear_conductance,
  measurement_model = mlpe,
  probe_maxit       = 2,
  verbose           = TRUE
)

assessment_spline

The printed summary will include a note such as:

Notes:
  - Smooth conductance formula expanded to 5 conductance parameters across
    1 smooth term (4 spline basis columns).

This confirms that the formula was evaluated on the expanded design matrix (one linear forest-cover column plus four altitude basis columns = five parameters total), and that probes were run with that full parameter count.

Spline expansions can make extreme starting values more risky because the expanded linear predictor is exponentiated into conductance. If a spline fit stops with a CHOLMOD warning or a non-positive-definite reduced Laplacian message, first scale the input rasters, restart with coefficients closer to zero, and check the retained graph cells for missing or infinite covariate values. During diagnosis, rerun terradish_assess_settings(..., verbose = TRUE) and consider solver = "auto" or solver = "amg" to separate direct-solver factorization problems from the conductance specification.

4.1 Passing $recommended to each candidate fit

When the assessment recommends a non-default optimizer (e.g. bfgs instead of newton because the parameter count after expansion is high), apply it to all candidate models to keep comparisons fair:

# Extract recommended optimizer and control once
rec <- assessment_spline$recommended

fit_ns_assessed <- terradish(
  melip.Fst ~ forestcover + s(altitude, df = 4),
  data              = surface,
  conductance_model = smooth_loglinear_conductance,
  measurement_model = mlpe,
  optimizer         = rec$optimizer,
  control           = rec$control,
  solver            = rec$solver,
  solver_control    = rec$solver_control
)

For this vignette we run a lightweight assessment (probe_maxit = 1) and reuse its recommendations across the candidate fits so the workflow is exercised in real code while staying fast.

5 Model fitting: from linear to spline

We fit four candidate models, moving from most constrained to most flexible:

  1. Linear baseline: one parameter per covariate, standard log-linear
  2. Altitude spline (ns): altitude enters as a natural spline; forest cover remains linear
  3. Both splines (ns): both covariates enter as natural splines
  4. Altitude spline (bs): altitude as a B-spline; forest cover linear (for comparison with model 2)

The coarsened demonstration graph keeps these repeated fits practical. For an applied analysis, use a scientifically justified raster resolution and allow the optimizer to converge.

    "Both ns (df=4 each)"

Interpret this table as a first-pass diagnostic, not a definitive verdict:

  • ΔAICc below about 2: a common heuristic for similar relative support within this declared candidate set, not proof of equivalence.
  • Larger ΔAICc: progressively less support relative to the top-ranked model. Interpret the magnitude together with model complexity, stability, and the predeclared scientific hypotheses.

An AICc improvement should be evaluated alongside the stability and scientific plausibility of the marginal association. A spline with a lower AICc but an implausible shape (oscillating wildly, extreme at the edges) should be treated with skepticism.

9 Marginal conductance associations

Marginal plots vary one covariate while averaging over the observed values of the other model covariates. For spline models this is the primary diagnostic tool because the basis coefficients are not individually interpretable.

9.1 Natural vs. B-spline altitude association

Use marginal_covariates = "altitude" to extract only the altitude panel from each model, then combine the ggplot objects side-by-side with patchwork, if it is installed:

library(ggplot2)

p_ns <- plot(fit_ns_alt, type = "marginal", data = surface, n = 80,
             marginal_covariates = "altitude") +
  ggtitle("Altitude: ns (df = 4)")

p_bs <- plot(fit_bs_alt, type = "marginal", data = surface, n = 80,
             marginal_covariates = "altitude") +
  ggtitle("Altitude: bs (k = 4)")

# patchwork is optional; fall back to one panel per page without it
if (requireNamespace("patchwork", quietly = TRUE)) {
  library(patchwork)
  p_ns | p_bs
} else {
  print(p_ns)
  print(p_bs)
}
Altitude marginal association on the conductance scale. Left: natural spline (ns). Right: B-spline (bs). Both use df/k = 4 and degree 3.

Broad agreement between spline families is a useful stability check, but it does not prove that a shape is genuine. Large discrepancies between natural and B-spline estimates at the same df/k indicate sensitivity to the basis choice; a simpler model or more constrained candidate set may be preferable.

9.3 Response-scale marginal associations

The type = "marginal_response" plot maps the same fitted association to the response scale. For the MLPE fits in this vignette, that scale is pairwise genetic distance. The plot includes approximate predictive uncertainty bands and can be easier to interpret than log conductance.

Comparing the ns and bs altitude response-scale associations side-by-side gives an additional stability check. Agreement does not establish causality or a unique ecological mechanism; divergence indicates sensitivity to the spline basis.

p_resp_ns <- plot(fit_ns_alt, type = "marginal_response", data = surface,
                  n = 80, marginal_covariates = "altitude") +
  ggtitle("ns (df = 4): response scale")

p_resp_bs <- plot(fit_bs_alt, type = "marginal_response", data = surface,
                  n = 80, marginal_covariates = "altitude") +
  ggtitle("bs (k = 4): response scale")

9.4 Robust tails with focal-support clamping

Spline models are especially sensitive to sparse support at covariate tails. To keep spline marginals and predicted conductance maps anchored to focal-site support, use support = "focal" with quantile bounds:

plot(
  fit_ns_alt,
  type = "marginal",
  data = surface,
  n = 80,
  support = "focal",
  support_probs = c(0.01, 0.99),
  clamp_covariates = "altitude"
)

cond_ns_alt_focal <- conductance(
  surface,
  fit_ns_alt,
  support = "focal",
  support_probs = c(0.01, 0.99),

11 Estimated conductance surfaces

The conductance surface shows the per-cell conductance value across the landscape. conductance() returns a three-layer SpatRaster with a point estimate and pointwise 95% confidence bounds from the delta method. These bounds reflect uncertainty in \(\theta\) conditional on the fitted model; they do not include nuisance-parameter or model-selection uncertainty.

14 Mixing linear and spline terms

You can freely mix linear terms and spline terms in the same formula. This is often the most useful specification: use a spline for the covariate you have prior reason to believe has a non-linear association, and keep all others linear to avoid over-parameterization.

16 Practical recommendations

16.1 When to use spline conductance

Use smooth_loglinear_conductance() when:

  1. There is a prior ecological reason to expect a non-linear landscape association, e.g. an intermediate optimum, a threshold, or a well-documented hump-shaped response in the literature.
  2. The linear model’s marginal association is implausible: e.g. a strong negative altitude coefficient that would predict near-zero conductance at high elevations where sampling actually occurs.
  3. The design can support the extra parameters. Pairwise observations are dependent, so do not justify spline complexity by counting all site pairs as independent replicates. Evaluate stability, focal-site support, AICc under its stated convention, and simulation under plausible scenarios.

16.2 When to stay with the linear model

  • Designs with sparse focal-site or covariate support relative to the proposed spline complexity.
  • No prior hypothesis about a non-linear association; treat splines as exploratory rather than confirmatory.
  • The spline and linear models give similar AICc; parsimony favors the simpler model.

16.4 Checking for overfitting

Even when AICc improves, inspect these warning signs:

  • Wild oscillation in the marginal curve, especially with sign reversals at the edges of the covariate range.
  • Very large basis coefficients relative to their standard errors.
  • Near-singular design matrix or optimizer warnings about convergence.
  • Large discrepancy between ns and bs at the same df/k: if the two spline families give qualitatively different shapes, neither has strong support.
# Quick eigenvalue check: are estimated parameters degenerate?
hess <- fit_ns_alt$fit$hessian
ev <- eigen(hess, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
min(ev)   # Should be clearly positive; near-zero → numerical problems

# Compare ns and bs marginal shapes side by side
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
plot(fit_ns_alt, type = "marginal", data = surface, main = "ns")
plot(fit_bs_alt, type = "marginal", data = surface, main = "bs")
